The file bites a bright curl from the blank, and already the bench complicates any later claim that metal is passive matter. A cut is a scar the steel must carry; the swarf preserves the file’s depth, though not its purpose. The sentence about key and pick smuggles in an author before it describes the mechanism. A warded lock has nowhere to record intent: the keyway admits a profile, each ward’s shoulder must pass its flank, the tumblers ride their springs, and the levers must gather enough lift to meet at the shear line. At that scale, key and pick are distinctions the lock cannot hear. The pickman gauges depthing by feel, searching for the sequence in which one ward drops and another rises; a rake substitutes repetition for exact profile. Either can satisfy the geometry the lock tests, though the pick may do it one ward at a time, and the bolt opens to geometry alone. The bench leaves a more ambiguous residue. A key worked repeatedly may polish a ward’s flank, while a pick can leave a bright, torn scar where the same travel crossed. Those marks are not testimony about identity. They describe contact: angle, pressure, edge, repetition. A later locksmith may guess from their width and polish which contour made them, but the scar itself cannot name the implement, still less the hand. Even the field lock’s forty-year weather sharpens the point: its worn notches have altered the friction and clearance that will accept the next profile. The witness log is prediction, not record; wear says that a certain geometry has acted here before and may act again, while the changed substrate decides anew what will open. After the lever falls back, the honest pause is the bench’s product: no hidden authority has spoken, only several possible cuts still waiting in the metal. Wear records passage, not permission, and the lock continues to open without learning who passed.
